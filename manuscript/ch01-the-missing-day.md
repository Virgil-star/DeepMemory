# HEIR ÆTERNA · BOOK ONE: THE INHERITANCE

## Chapter One — The Missing Day

The first thing Iris ever stole was a Tuesday.

She found it folded between two pages of her father's atlas, where the oceans were
painted blue, the boundaries of dead countries were drawn in gray, and a small
handwritten note had been pressed into the binding.

*This was the day you were born.*

Her mother's handwriting.

Iris read it three times.

There had been no Tuesdays for nineteen years. Children learned this before they
learned their multiplication tables. The week contained six days, the calendar had
always contained six days, and anybody who remembered otherwise was advised to
make an appointment with the Ministry of Continuity.

Her father had once told her that impossible things were merely expensive.

She wondered how much he had paid for her.

---

The atlas lived on the third shelf, which was the shelf she had been able to reach
since she was eleven, and which was therefore not a hiding place. That was the
first thing she thought, standing in his study with the rain going sideways past
the window: he had not hidden it. He had simply put it somewhere she had never had
a reason to look.

It was an enormous book. The spine had been rebacked twice, badly, with glue that
had gone the colour of weak tea, and the whole thing had to be carried in both
arms like a sleeping animal. She had opened it perhaps four times in her life and
always for the pictures — the great blue spreads of the oceans, the mountains done
in little brown hatchings, the names of cities set in a typeface nobody used
anymore.

She had never once wondered why so many of the borders were gray.

They were gray in a way that looked deliberate rather than faded. Firm lines,
evenly inked, enclosing shapes that had names printed inside them in small
capitals, and she could not have told anyone which countries those were. Not one.
She had sat eleven years of geography. She could draw the eastern coast from
memory. But there was a gray country north of the strait that she had absolutely
never heard of in her life, and its capital was marked with a star, and the star
had a dot of something pale and dried beside it that might have been tea or might
have been nothing.

Iris looked at that for a while longer than she meant to.

Then she went back to the thing in her hands.

It was a day-slip. She knew that the way she knew a bus token or a ration coupon —
from the back of a classroom, from a poster in the Registry, from the general
atmosphere of adult life. Continuity stock, stiff as a playing card and about
twice the size, the colour of old cream. Watermarked. When she tilted it toward
the window the Ministry's seal swam up out of the paper, a plain circle with a
number inside it, and then sank away again.

Across the top, in letterpress, so crisp she could feel the bite of it with her
thumb:

**BEARER · ONE DAY · TWENTY-FOUR HOURS · UNLIVED**

And under that, a serial.

Every child in the city could read a serial. They taught it at nine, in the same
week they taught you how to check a banknote, with the same cheerful
poster-and-pointer thoroughness: three letters, then eight digits, and the letters
had to come from the issue range, and the issue range was published, and the
published range for the last four years began with the letter D.

The letters on her father's slip were HEX.

Iris turned it over. The back was blank except for a printed line, no larger than
the print in a hymnal, which read: *Issued against reserve. Not transferable to a
named holder.* She read it twice and understood it the way you understand a
sentence in a language you have only had one year of — the words arrived, the
meaning did not.

Downstairs the front door went.

She had, she estimated, eleven seconds. She used four of them standing perfectly
still with the slip in her hand and the atlas open across both forearms, and in
those four seconds she decided something that she would spend the next seven years
failing to undecide.

She put the note in the pocket of her cardigan.

She put the slip in her shoe.

She put the atlas back on the third shelf with the rebacked spine facing out, the
way it had been, and she was on the stairs with her geography folder against her
chest by the time her father had got his coat off.

"You're wet," she said.

"It's wet out," said Edmund Rask, and hung the coat on the second peg, and put his
bag down against the wall in the place where it went. "There's fish."

---

The house was narrow and warm and smelled of coal gas and the particular dust that
settles on hot metal. It had four rooms that mattered and two that did not, and a
back door that had not opened in Iris's lifetime because the step had subsided. Her
father cooked badly with enormous attention. Tinned fish, potatoes, a dab of
something sharp out of a jar. He laid the table properly, two of everything, even
when he was eating standing up at the counter reading, which was most nights.

Tonight he sat.

"Three," he said.

It was the oldest game in the house and she could not remember the start of it. He
asked three questions about her day. She asked one about his. The asymmetry had
been explained to her once, when she was small, as the natural result of her day
being more interesting, and she had believed it for about nine years.

"Go on, then."

"Did you eat at lunch."

"Yes."

"What."

"Bread and the egg. Bo had crisps and I had four of them."

"Four is a reasonable number of crisps." He turned a potato over with his fork,
inspecting it the way he inspected everything, as though it might have a serial on
it somewhere. "Third. Did anything happen."

This was the question. It was always the third question and it was always phrased
exactly that way, and she had answered it perhaps four thousand times with
*nothing*, and she had never once before tonight understood that it was a question
somebody might be asking for a reason.

"Nothing," said Iris.

"Good," said her father.

She watched him eat for a moment. He was fifty-eight and had the long hands and
the slight permanent stoop of a man who had spent his working life reading
documents at a desk a little too low for him. There was grey at his temples and
none anywhere else, which she had always found faintly comic, as if he had been
greying on a schedule and the schedule had been interrupted.

"My turn," she said.

"Your turn."

"Was there ever a seventh day?"

Her father did not stop eating. That was the thing she noticed afterward, lying
awake: he did not stop eating, he did not look up, he did not do any of the six or
seven things a person does when a question lands somewhere tender. He finished
the mouthful he was working on, and set the fork down on the rest, and said:

"The calendar has always contained six days."

It was the sentence off the poster. Word for word, in the order the poster had it,
with the small pause before *always* that the recording in the school hall put
there too. Iris had heard it from the Ministry, from teachers, from the back of a
tram ticket, from a woman on the wireless with a lovely voice. She had never once
heard it from her father, and hearing it in his mouth was like hearing him speak
in somebody else's accent.

"Why do you ask?" he said.

And she lied.

It was not a good lie. She said she had seen something in a book, which was true,
and she said she could not remember which book, which was not, and the whole
construction had the wobble of a chair with one leg short. She listened to herself
building it and she was ashamed of it even as she laid the last piece on.

Her father let her finish.

Then he picked up his fork again and said, "Which book?"

"The atlas."

"Mm," said Edmund Rask.

He ate three more pieces of potato. The gas hissed in the ring behind him. Out in
the street somebody's bicycle went past with a bad bearing, two notes, over and
over, fading up the hill.

"That atlas," he said, "is older than the Ministry."

He said it mildly, the way he said that bread was stale or that a coat was wet. And
it was not an answer, and it was not a lie, and it was not an accident, and Iris
understood with a cold clarity that was entirely new to her that her father had
just told her something enormous on purpose, and that he would never in his life
admit that he had done it.

"Eat," he said. "You've gone pale."

---

He never lied to her. She thought about that in bed, with the lamp off and the
slip under her pillow because her shoe had started to feel ridiculous.

Not once. Not about her mother, not about his work, not about the money, not about
the time she had asked him at eight years old whether people could be made to stop
existing and he had looked at her for a long moment and said *yes*, and then
answered every single question she had after that, for an hour, with the lamp on,
until she ran out of them and fell asleep against his arm.

He had never lied. He had simply arranged, with enormous care, over sixteen years,
to be asked the right questions.

Iris lay in the dark and performed an unpleasant piece of arithmetic.

She had asked whether there was ever a seventh day. He had said the calendar had
always contained six. Those two statements, laid side by side, did not touch each
other at any point. And she had been told both of them her whole life, from the
same posters, in the same hall, by the same kind voices — *the calendar has always
contained six days*, and *there have been no Tuesdays for nineteen years* — and
she had held them both in her head since she was five years old without ever once
putting them in the same sentence.

Nineteen years ago something had stopped happening.

And something had always been that way.

They could not both be true. Somebody had decided they should both be taught
anyway, and the something they covered between them was the exact size and shape
of a day.

She got up and lit the lamp and took out the slip.

**BEARER · ONE DAY · TWENTY-FOUR HOURS · UNLIVED**

HEX 41660903.

She wrote the serial down in the back of her geography notebook, in her own
handwriting, small, and then — because she wanted it somewhere other than in a
pocket that went through the wash — she copied out the note as well.

*This was the day you were born.*

Her own hand was nothing like her mother's. She had seen the difference since she
was old enough to want to see it. Her mother's letters leaned forward and ran
together and finished their words in a hurry, as if the writing were only a
transcription of something already said out loud and the speaker had moved on. Iris
wrote upright and separate and slow. She had tried, at twelve, in secret, for about
five months, to make her writing go like her mother's, and had got nowhere, and had
stopped, and had never told anybody she had tried.

She looked at the two lines on the page. Her mother's, pressed thin by sixteen
years in a binding. Hers, fresh, in pencil, upright.

Then she turned the lamp off.

---

What she knew about her mother, complete, in the order she had learned it:

Her name was Thea. She had dark hair, from the one photograph, which was indoors
and badly lit and showed her laughing at somebody who was not in the frame. She
was not dead — her father had been very precise about that, every time, with a
precision Iris had taken for comfort and had lately begun to find strange. She had
gone away when Iris was two. She was not coming back. She had not been taken, she
had not been lost, she had not left a note, and she was not dead.

That was the whole of it. Six facts in sixteen years, and the sixth one — *not
dead* — had been repeated so often and so exactly that Iris, lying in the dark at
sixteen, finally heard it for what it was.

Nobody says *not dead* about the living.

---

In the morning there was an envelope in the post slot.

She came down first, as she did every day, because her father liked twenty minutes
alone with the wireless and she liked twenty minutes alone with the kettle, and the
post came early. There were two things on the mat. One was a Ministry circular
about the winter fuel allocation, buff, with the circle-and-number seal in the
corner.

The other was a letter addressed to her.

Iris did not get letters. She stood in the cold hall in her socks and picked it up
and turned it over and the first thing she registered, before the stamp and before
the postmark and before anything, was that she had addressed it herself.

Not somebody with handwriting like hers. *Hers.* The upright separate letters, the
particular way her capital I came out slightly too narrow, the habit she had never
managed to break of closing the loop on the second s in her own surname. Sixteen
years of exercise books. She would have known it across a room.

The hand was older. That was the only difference, and it was not a small one: the
letters were the same letters, formed the same way, by someone who had been forming
them for a very long time. They had the economy of a signature. Somewhere between
the girl standing in the hall and the person who had written her name on this
envelope, forty years of writing had happened, and all of it had gone the way hers
was already going.

There was no return address.

The stamp was pale green and showed a bridge she did not recognise, over a river
she did not recognise, and the country name along the bottom edge was printed in
small capitals, and she had never heard of it, and she knew — standing in the hall,
in her socks, with the fuel circular in her other hand — exactly where she had seen
it before. On the third shelf. North of the strait. Enclosed in a firm gray line.

And the postmark, struck clean and black across the bridge, gave a date she read
four times before she accepted it.

It was forty-three years from Thursday.

Upstairs the wireless came on, and her father's voice said something to it, the way
he did, mildly, as if it could hear him.

Iris put the circular on the hall table where the post went.

Then she put the letter inside her geography folder, between the folder's cardboard
back and the last page of her notebook, where the serial was, and she carried it to
school, and she did not open it for two days.
