# HEIR ÆTERNA · BOOK THREE

# THE APPEAL

*What am I going to do about it?* — Thirty-four chapters. Nineteen days. Iris is
twenty-three.

**Mode: stripped.** The shortest and fastest volume. Fewest settings, fewest named
characters, and **no world-explanation of any kind** — by now the reader knows the
machinery, and anything restated is a failure of nerve. Four locations total: the
Office of Claims, her father's house, Pell's laundry, the Foundry.

**She becomes:** an author.
**It costs her:** her father. His custodianship, and then his memory of her.

---

## PART ONE — The Refusal (1–11)

**1. Restored**
Thea Moravec's testimony in trust is entered to authenticate her daughter's
provenance. A dead woman from an unobtaining history restores Iris's standing, and
the restoration takes four minutes and a stamp.

**2. The Form**
It is two pages. The second page is the one that matters.

**3. Grandin's Kindness**
He explains it exactly, kindly, and without pressure, and shows her where to sign,
and brings her a glass of water. The Ministry at its most humane is the most
frightening it has been in three books.

**4. Refusal**
She refuses. Not a speech. A blank line on a form, and a clerk's small intake of
breath.

**5. The Consequence of a Blank Line**
Settlement proceeds. The losses begin arriving within the hour: small, local,
irreversible, and billed to her.

**6. Bo**
Bo Teague's family. The novel pays this on the page, in full, and does not cut
away. He does not know her at the end of the chapter, and does not know that he
used to.

**7. The First Street**
Her school. The corner where she bought bread for nine years. An absence that
nobody but she can perceive as an absence.

**8. Her Father's Plea**
He begs. He has never begged, in two and a half books, and she has to watch him
discover that he is capable of it.

**9. The Offer**
The administration's counter: certify, and the Fourth Concordance's surviving
record is preserved in the archive in perpetuity. Her mother's world offered to
her as a bribe, by an institution that does not experience this as cruelty.

**10. Mireille's Side**
Mireille Dax asks her to sign. The woman whose world it is tells Iris to certify
it out of existence, because she will not be saved by somebody else's child and
she has a daughter who needs a mother who can look at herself.
*Cost: the one person with standing to absolve her refuses to.*

**11. The Wider Filing** — *part turn*
Rather than certify, she files the continuity injunction against the reconciliation
process itself.

---

## PART TWO — The Appeal (12–24)

**12. Void**
The ground: the Reconciler's mandate rests on consent that was inherited, and
therefore void by the system's own validity check.

**13. Inherited Consent**
The founding generation accepted the terms on behalf of everyone who came after.
Postulate IV says consent cannot be inherited. The argument is three sentences
long and takes eleven years of other people's work to make admissible.

**14. The Ledger, Entered**
Clerke's evidence — including the line Iris traded at sixteen, which now arrives
in the record with her own fingerprints on its provenance. She does not get to
pretend it came from anywhere else.

**15. Thea's Testimony, Entered**
Her mother's case, filed at last by the executor it was left to.

**16. The Reconciler Does Not Argue**
It adjudicates. Numbered findings, quoted as documents, in present tense. It is
scrupulously fair and it is the most frightening thing in the trilogy for exactly
that reason.

**17. Her Father Is Called**
To testify against her, because preserving his world requires it. He accepts the
summons. He tells her he will accept it before he does, which is the closest thing
to tenderness available.

**18. His Testimony**
He gives it, completely, and well.

**19. The Undercut**
He undercuts himself in one answer — a qualification nobody in the room has the
standing to notice except his daughter. She notices. He will not admit to it
afterward, in this volume or ever.

**20. Suspension**
The injunction holds. Unilateral revision stops across every Concordance. Nothing
is saved, nothing is restored, and nothing is undone. A hold is not a victory and
the chapter refuses to let it feel like one.

**21. The Docket**
Erased histories entitled to contest, listed in order of filing. Three names on
the list the reader has met as local colour: the woman from the tenement, Bo
Teague, Mireille Dax. Nothing is announced. The list is the payoff.

**22. What She Cannot Save**
The enumerated unsaved, named aloud in a room with nobody comforted: not the
Fourth Concordance, not the Tuesdays, not the tenement woman's month, not Bo's
family.

**23. The Provenance Archive**
Looking for the origin of the Foundry. The atlas is entered into evidence here —
its gray boundaries are the only surviving cartography of four unobtaining
histories, which is why her father kept it, which she only now understands.

**24. The Proof** — *part turn*
She reads the anonymous proof that made the machine possible and recognises the
hand. It is hers. She has not written it yet. **No character glosses this, here or
anywhere.**

---

## PART THREE — The Choice (25–34)

**25. Three Doors**
Submit it unchanged. Submit it altered. Refuse. She works out all three herself,
and nobody tells her which to take, because nobody can.

**26. The Price of Each**
Unchanged: the loop closes and she becomes the author of everything she has spent
three books indicting. Altered: a weaker Foundry, later, and whatever the lost
years bought is unbought. Refused: no machine, no settlements, and no way to know
which coherent history the structure selects instead — possibly kinder, possibly
without any of them.

**27. Pell's Price**
To stand in the Foundry during a settlement she needs hours nobody has allocated.
Pell will sell them, and tells her, as always and without being asked, exactly
whose they are. A name, an address, a reason the hours were sold.

**28. She Buys Them**
The trilogy pays this on the page and does not look away. It is the last thing she
does that is unambiguously wrong, and she does not excuse it afterward.

**29. Standing in the Foundry**
Alone, inside bought time, with the proof and a pen.

**30. The Altered Proof**
She writes it. The injunction goes in as a mathematical necessity rather than an
administrative courtesy: no administration can suspend it, because the machine
cannot exist without it.

**31. Eleven Years**
The price, itemised: eleven years of the Foundry's early interventions never
happen.

**32. Settlement**
Present tense. Years pass in a page. The structure reconciles and the losses
arrive exactly as billed — the early interventions gone, and with them her
father's custodianship, and with that his memory of her.

**33. Distributed Custodianship**
The new arrangement, and not a victory. Unilateral revision suspended. Surviving
evidence preserved. Custody distributed among claimants. A docket of erased
histories now entitled to contest, which is decades of work for other people.

The Reconciler is not dismantled. It is **re-employed** — reassigned as registrar
of the contests it used to foreclose, still scrupulous, now working for the
claimants. The most feared instrument in the world, given a new office.

Iris is offered a seat on the adjudicating body. **She refuses it.** One person
holding the authority because somebody had to is precisely her father, and the
refusal is the hardest thing she does and the one thing he could never have done.

The volume's last argument, raised late by a minor character and never answered:
whether any of this is a break from reciprocal bookkeeping, or only better
paperwork.

**34. The Day You Were Born**
Edmund Rask, no longer a custodian: a man with a good mind and a thin file, in a
small flat. Iris visits. He does not know her. He is courteous, and curious about
her work, and offers her tea.

On the table the oversized atlas lies open, the boundaries of unobtaining
countries drawn in gray, and pressed into the binding, in a hand he cannot place,
a note that reads *This was the day you were born.*

She puts it back.

She does not forgive him, and she does not condemn him, and she does not complete
the future he imagined for her. She finishes her tea, and goes back to work.

## Closing obligations

**Every outstanding bill paid:** the tenement woman's month (ch. 22, unpaid and
named as unpayable), the orphan serial (ch. 14), Bo Teague (ch. 6, ch. 21),
Mireille (ch. 10, ch. 21), the ledger line Iris traded at sixteen (ch. 14), the
staircase drawing (Book Two ch. 11), the letter (ch. 24, read correctly at last).

**The one permitted recovery across all three books:** the record of the Fourth
Concordance, preserved on the docket — information, not matter. Thea stays dead.

**The cycle closes line for line.** Chapter one of Book One and chapter
thirty-four of Book Three are the same atlas, the same hand, the same gesture,
inverted. She stole it at sixteen. At twenty-three she leaves it where it is.
