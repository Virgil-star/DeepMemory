# HEIR ÆTERNA

## Craft research — what the best trilogies do, and what we take

Six models interrogated for structural technique rather than subject matter. Each
entry states the finding, then the change it forces on *Heir Æterna*. Findings
that did not survive contact with our material are recorded as rejected, because
a research note that approves of everything is worthless.

**On source quality, stated plainly:** the open web on this subject is mostly
practitioner blogs and study guides. The load-bearing findings below are the
Oresteia's re-employment of the Furies, Beckett's authorship fold, and Mantel's
tense argument. The middle-book material is practitioner consensus and is treated
as such. For citable scholarship on the Areopagus scene or on Mantel's present
tense, a university edition and Kucała's Cambridge chapter respectively would be
the next step.

---

## 1. Aeschylus, *Oresteia* — the trilogy that ends by inventing a court

The foundational model, and the closest structural relative we have. A cycle of
inherited guilt in the House of Atreus, where archaic reciprocal justice means
every act of retribution is itself a new crime. Orestes is pursued by the Furies.
The resolution is institutional: Athena convenes a tribunal instead of ruling on
the case herself, and the Furies are not defeated but given a new office as the
Eumenides. Critically, the serious reading of the trilogy leaves open whether the
court is a genuine break from reciprocal violence or its continuation in better
clothes.

Our subject is already this: inherited obligation, a world running on
retributive bookkeeping, resolved by the invention of an adjudicative body. Three
changes follow.

**Change 1 — the Reconciler is re-employed, not destroyed.**
Previously Book Three dismantled the mandate. Wrong, and smaller. The Furies are
not killed; they are given the docket. The Reconciler survives the finding that
voids its mandate and is reassigned as the registrar of the very contests it used
to foreclose — the most feared instrument in the world, still scrupulous, now
working for the claimants. Nothing is wasted and nothing is forgiven.

**Change 2 — Iris must not be the judge.**
Athena convenes and then steps back. Iris was drifting toward becoming the
arbiter of the new system, which would make her exactly her father: one person
holding the authority because somebody had to. She files, she establishes
standing for others, and she refuses a seat. The refusal of power is the
character's last and hardest act, and it is the thing her father could never do.

**Change 3 — the ending stays open on its own legitimacy.**
Distributed custodianship must not be presented as justice achieved. The last
movement should admit the possibility that it is reciprocal bookkeeping with
better paperwork, and decline to settle it. One line of doubt, placed late, and
never answered.

## 2. Beckett, *Molloy* / *Malone Dies* / *The Unnamable* — proceeding by collapse

The trilogy strips a layer per volume: plot largely gone by the second, character
reduced to a dying man in a bed, and by the third little but bare voice. Kunkel's
formulation is that it proceeds by way of collapse. Two specifics matter to us.

**The authorship fold.** The final narrator claims to have authored the
protagonists of the two previous volumes. Molloy and Malone fold back into one
voice. This is structurally identical to our climax — Iris reads the proof that
made her father's machine possible and recognises her own hand — which tells us
the move is sound and, more usefully, that it is *known*. A known move needs
executing with confidence, not explaining with anxiety. Cut any paragraph in
which a character glosses the loop for the reader.

**Progressive stripping, formally and not only emotionally.** Our cost ladder
already removes a support per volume: the house, the mother, the father. The
Beckett model says the *form* must thin to match. Book Three therefore runs
shortest, with the fewest settings, the fewest named characters, and no
world-explanation whatsoever. By then the reader knows the machinery; anything
restated is a failure of nerve.

**The gap that resolves nothing.** Coe's observation that Malone's death solves
precisely nothing is the rule for our volume breaks. Book One answers *what am I*
informationally and resolves nothing emotionally. The seam between volumes is a
wound, not a hinge.

## 3. Mantel, Wolf Hall trilogy — tense as an argument about the past

Mantel's present tense is the trilogy's defining move: she wanted the jitter and
flux of events as lived, against what she called the knowingness of past tense,
and critics read it as making the historical past feel contingent rather than
settled. Her close third keeps Cromwell as "he," never leaving his vantage. The
volumes are continuous, *The Mirror and the Light* opening on the beheading that
closed *Bring Up the Bodies*.

This is the most consequential craft decision in our project, because our
*premise* is that the past is contingent rather than settled. Mantel's technique
is almost our thesis statement.

**Rejected, with reasons.** We do not convert to present tense. The author's own
opening — *The first thing Iris ever stole was a Tuesday* — is past, and it is
right, because in this novel the retrospective voice is not knowingness. It is
**testimony**. The entire trilogy builds toward Iris giving evidence on the
record, and a narration that reads as deposition is thematically load-bearing in
a way present tense would destroy. Past tense here means *this has been entered
into evidence*, which is the opposite of settled: it is contested.

**Adopted as a hybrid.** Present tense is reserved, strictly, for two registers:

- the Reconciler's numbered findings, quoted as documents
- the settlement sequences, where history is actively being chosen

The effect is that present tense marks the live wire. When a chapter slips into
present, the past has come unfixed. Used perhaps six times across three books.

**Adopted wholesale.** Close third on Iris, never leaving her vantage, in all
three volumes, with no exceptions beyond the quoted documents. Already our rule;
Mantel confirms it is survivable across a trilogy of this length.

## 4. Kieślowski, *Three Colours* — connective tissue that is not plot

The films share no storyline. They are bound by a thematic scheme, by motif, by a
score that crosses between them, and by a single reveal withheld until the end of
the third film, where the principals of all three turn out to be survivors of the
same ferry disaster. The famous accident of the titles — they are the colours of
the French flag because France paid for them, and Kieślowski said that German
money would have made them Black, Red and Yellow — is a useful reminder that a
unifying scheme can be arbitrary in origin and still do real structural work.

**Change 4 — the shared-disaster reveal.**
Three apparently minor figures, one introduced per volume, converge in Book
Three's final filing as named claimants on the docket: the woman in the tenement
whose month Iris spent in Book One, Bo Teague from Book One's ordinary world, and
Mireille Dax of the Ninth Concordance from Book Two. The reader has met each as
local colour. They turn out to be the case. Nothing about this is announced; they
simply appear on a list, in order, and the list is the payoff.

**Change 5 — a unifying title scheme.**
Our book titles were descriptive. Replace with three legal instruments, escalating
by what Iris is in each: *The Inheritance* — what she receives. *The Instrument* —
what she is used as. *The Appeal* — what she files. One register, three steps,
and the middle title indicts her father in a single word.

**Rejected.** Kieślowski's volumes are thematically rather than serially linked.
We are serial and should stay serial; a Heir Æterna where the three books share
only a theme would waste the cost ladder.

## 5. Mahfouz, *Cairo Trilogy* — deliberate gaps and the time-marker object

Presented as distinct chunks rather than one continuous whole: roughly 1917–19,
then a jump to 1924–27, then 1935–44. The opening volume moves slowly through
household routine and apparently unchanging daily life. *Palace of Desire* opens
on the patriarch taking out a gold watch — a time marker doing the work of
signalling a changed tempo. By the end, whole years pass quickly. The family
supplies the structure, the plot being the interrelation of its members.

**Change 6 — Book Two opens on a time-marker object.**
Our gaps are already Mahfouzian: one autumn at sixteen, four diffuse years from
nineteen, nineteen days at twenty-three. What was missing was the gold watch. Book
Two therefore opens on a day-slip with a **sequential** serial — issued stock,
bought legally, over a counter, with a receipt. One object tells the reader that
three years have passed and that the girl who stole a cut slip out of an atlas now
transacts inside the system. No summary required.

**Change 7 — late compression.**
The settlement sequence may pass years in a page, and should. Mahfouz earns this
by having moved slowly first; our Book One's episodic autumn is what buys Book
Three's compression.

## 6. Ray, *Apu Trilogy* — episodic to driven, and the recurring object

A continuous timeline from a boy's childhood to his fatherhood. The second film
substitutes the episodic nature of the first with a strong narrative. Kemp's BFI
study identifies an overarching cyclical structure as the source of the trilogy's
effect. The railway recurs across all three films with what one critic calls the
palpable consistency of an integral element of the design. Ray did not plan a
trilogy; the idea was suggested to him at Venice after the second film.

**Change 8 — permission for Book One to be episodic, and a prohibition on Book Two.**
Book One is a sixteen-year-old poking at a world, and may be episodic: discrete
incursions, each one a chapter, loosely strung. Book Two may not. From its first
page it is driven, with a single escalating line of pursuit. This resolves our
middle-volume risk structurally rather than by exhortation.

**Change 9 — the atlas is our railway.**
One object recurs with palpable consistency across all three volumes: the
oversized atlas with the boundaries of unobtaining countries drawn in gray. It
appears in Book One chapter 1, is consulted in Book Two, is entered as evidence in
Book Three, and sits on the table in the coda. Four appearances, no symbolism
announced.

**Change 10 — the cycle closes line for line.**
The coda rhymes with chapter one, deliberately and almost phrase for phrase, with
the gesture inverted: she stole the note from the binding at sixteen, and at the
end she puts it back and leaves it. Same atlas, same hand, opposite act.

## 7. Middle-volume consensus — and our test

Practitioner advice converges tightly: give the middle volume its own arc rather
than bridge material; open from a raised problem instead of an introduction; make
each turn worsen the situation; escalate so that the third volume becomes
inevitable; end less stable than you began; and never let the trilogy decompose
into exposition, rising action, climax. The most bracing version of the advice is
that a middle volume with no reason to exist means the story needed two books.

**The test applied to Book Two, honestly.** It has its own antagonist (the
programme, not the administration), its own climax (her standing suspended on a
fabrication challenge, then restored by her dead mother's testimony), and its own
irreversible cost (the end of rescue). It opens on a raised problem and ends with
her legally precarious rather than merely troubled. It passes.

**Change 11 — one strengthening.** Book Two's close was the certification demand
arriving. That is a hook, not an instability. Reordered: the fabrication challenge
suspends her standing *before* the demand lands, so the volume ends with the
administration requiring a certification from a woman it has just ruled may not
exist. That is less stable than it began, which is the whole of the advice.

## Rejected

- Present-tense narration throughout (Mantel) — past tense is testimony here, and
  the author's opening line is already right.
- Thematic-only linkage between volumes (Three Colours) — we are serial, and the
  cost ladder depends on it.
- Collapse of character and plot as a formal programme (Beckett) — we strip
  supports, not legibility. This is a novel with a plot and must stay one.
