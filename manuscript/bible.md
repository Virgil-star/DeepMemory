# HEIR ÆTERNA

## Story Bible

*Heir Æterna* — the eternal heir, and the heir to
eternity. She inherits a world she never agreed to, and in the end she files an
appeal against the thing that made it permanent.

FUTURESPEC · SPECULATIVE FICTION · TEMPORAL ONTOLOGY · HUMAN SOVEREIGNTY

> **A trilogy.** This file governs all three volumes — *The Inheritance*,
> *The Instrument*, *The Appeal*. Series architecture in `trilogy.md`; the craft
> models behind its revisions in `craft-research.md`.

## 0. The line and the thesis

**Logline.** In a late-twenty-first-century world where reality is a continuously
reconciled structure rather than a fixed past, the man who invented historical
revision fathers a child with a woman from an erased history so that his own
world cannot legally be deleted — and his daughter grows up to file the one
claim he never insured against: an appeal against the whole process.

**Thesis.** The inventor believed he could save reality by creating someone who
would never permit it to change. Instead he created the first person with the
standing and the courage to demand that reality justify itself.

**What the novel is actually about.** Inherited obligation. The father's error is
assuming continuity and goodness are the same thing. The daughter's discovery is
that a world can be worth saving without belonging to the person who saved it.
The mother's insistence is that the future must belong, at least in part, to
people who have not yet been born.

## 1. The four postulates

These are fictional postulates, not physics. They earn their keep by being
consistent enough to drive the plot and restrictive enough that no character can
escape a dilemma by inventing another machine. Each is stated with the thing it
**forbids the author**, because that is where the pressure comes from.

### I. Conservation of causal history

Changing the past creates no consequence-free future. Every revision displaces
possibility and leaves a measurable **causal debt**, denominated in displaced
hours. Reality selects one coherent history; the alternatives are not destroyed,
they become **unobtaining** — recoverable as information, not as matter.

- Evidence survives. People do not.
- A recovered record can prove a person existed. It cannot return them.
- Debt is never forgiven, only carried, serviced, or transferred.

*Forbids:* resurrection, free undo, the clean reset. Anything the plot wants to
reverse must be paid for in displaced hours taken from someone named.

### II. Dual provenance

Iris inherits no temporal faculty. There is no gift, no sight, no immunity. Her
significance is documentary and biological at once: her parents hold
authenticated origins in two mutually incompatible histories, and the
administration's own continuity rules therefore recognise her claim in both.

- She is not special. She is **admissible**.
- Everything she achieves she achieves with ordinary instruments: patience,
  archives, nerve, theft, and the willingness to be disbelieved.

*Forbids:* powers. If a scene can only be solved by Iris being more than human,
the scene is wrong.

### III. The continuity injunction

A recognised person holding legitimate claims in competing histories may suspend
their unilateral deletion while those claims are adjudicated.

- It **suspends**. It does not reverse, restore, or compensate.
- It requires the claimant's living, uncoerced assent, renewed on the record.
- It lapses on the claimant's death, on proof of fabricated provenance, or on a
  finding of void testimony.
- It binds the reconciliation process only. It does not bind the universe, the
  weather, a bullet, or a man with a key to her room.
- It has only ever been filed on behalf of a single history. Filing it against
  the process itself is unprecedented, and that is the third act.

*Forbids:* invulnerability. Iris is a living obstacle, not an immortal one. She
can be killed, discredited, or simply outwaited.

### IV. Consent cannot be inherited

A parent may create the conditions of a child's existence. He cannot inherit her
decisions, compel her testimony, or transfer her right to refuse.

This is the one constraint the Foundry cannot circumvent, and it is enforced by
the Reconciler's own validity check rather than by anyone's conscience:

> **Coerced testimony is void, and void testimony collapses the claim it
> supports.**

The mechanic is the engine of the book. Force her and the injunction dies with
the forcing. Her father therefore has exactly two instruments — persuasion and
deception — and he spends sixteen years choosing the second one while telling
himself it is the first.

*Forbids:* the hostage plot. No antagonist can win by putting a gun to her head.
They must win by making her *want* to agree, or by arranging a world in which
agreeing is the only thing left.

## 2. The machinery

**The Foundry** (Chronosphere's World Foundry). The instrument that observes,
reconstructs and revises historical states. It does not send bodies to
yesterday. It submits a *candidate history* and lets the structure reconcile.
Travel is a side effect of revision, not its purpose. Operators speak of
"standing in" a history, never of visiting it.

**The Reconciler.** The autonomous temporal administration's adjudicating core.
Not a mind and not a person; it issues numbered **findings** in flat, courteous
prose and does not argue. It recognises claims, weighs causal strength, and
settles. It was given its mandate by the first generation of operators, who
accepted its terms on behalf of everyone who came after. That acceptance is the
hairline crack the novel eventually drives a wedge into.

**A Settlement.** A reconciliation event. Not scheduled — the Reconciler settles
when causal pressure exceeds tolerance. People learn afterward, if at all. The
numbering is public; the contents usually are not.

**Concordances.** Histories, numbered by the Reconciler in the order it
adjudicated them. The numbering is an administrative artefact that everyone
reads as a provenance. This mistake is the novel's central political fact.

- **First Concordance** — designated baseline. Known everywhere as **Reality
  Zero**: the history in which the Foundry was first built. Edmund Rask's
  assigned custody.
- **Fourth Concordance** — Thea Moravec's history. Settled out nineteen years
  before the novel opens but one Settlement earlier than the Tuesdays.
- **Sixth Settlement** — the one that took Tuesdays from the First Concordance
  to service a debt. Nineteen years ago.
- **Ninth Concordance** — the rival. A history whose causal claim measures
  stronger than Reality Zero's. Act III's pressure.

## 3. The Ministry of Continuity

The Reconciler's human-facing bureaucracy. Ordinary people meet the Ministry the
way they meet a tax office: rarely, politely, and with dread.

- **The Registry.** Documents. Issues and reissues the record of what happened.
  A citizen may request their own file. The Registry is where the novel's first
  crime is discovered, because the Registry is honest in a world that is not.
- **The Reallocator.** The instrument that moves hours. Takes lived time from
  where it can be spared and applies it where debt is owed. Abolishing Tuesdays
  was a Reallocator action: cheaper to take one weekday from everyone than to
  take a year from a few.
- **Audit.** Officers who reconcile lived time against logged time. An Auditor
  can tell, from a household's consumption, that someone in it has spent a day
  that does not exist.
- **The Office of Claims.** Adjudication. Where standing is proved, injunctions
  filed, and testimony recorded. Registrar of Claims: **Hollis Grandin**.
- **Custodial Services.** Licensed custodians, each assigned one reality to
  protect. A custodianship is a job with a pension and a conscience problem.

**Official doctrine on the week:** the calendar has always contained six days.
**Operational memory:** there have been no Tuesdays for nineteen years. Both
sentences are taught. Nobody holds them side by side. Chapter One is the moment
one child does.

## 4. The economy of hours

Lived time is fungible, and therefore it has a market.

- **Day-slip.** A bearer instrument for unlived time: printed on continuity
  stock, watermarked, serialised, denominated in a quantum of twenty-four hours,
  with no named holder. Whoever holds it, spends it.
- **Issued stock** carries sequential serials. **Cut slips** do not — their
  serials are orphans, and an orphan serial means the hours were cut out of
  someone living rather than allocated from reserve.
- **Butchers** do the cutting and sell the slips. **Pell** works out of a
  shuttered laundry and knows where erased people wash up, because the erased
  need somewhere to be and an unlived hour is the only address that will hold
  them.
- **Auditors** hunt orphan serials. **Sabine Clerke** is very good at it.
- Spending a slip puts the holder inside an hour the record does not contain.
  It is the only place in the world where a person from an unobtaining history
  can be met, spoken to, and remembered afterward.

A purchased Tuesday is therefore not a luxury. It is a visiting room.

## 5. The reproductive strategy

Edmund Rask's reasoning, stated plainly because the novel should never let him
hide behind complexity:

1. An object moved between histories is trivially isolated, classified and
   eliminated. So is an anomalous man; the Ministry already has a file reconciling
   him.
2. A child is neither. A child introduces new causal relationships and, decisively,
   an independent person holding claims that neither parent can exercise on her
   behalf.
3. Therefore: find a woman whose history has already been settled out, bring her
   into Reality Zero, and have a daughter with standing in both.

Thea Moravec understood every word of this and agreed to the daughter. She
refused the instrument. Her condition was an independently enforceable right of
refusal, lodged with the Office of Claims where Edmund could not reach it. He
agreed, in writing, and meant it.

Eleven years later he began quietly arranging the circumstances of his
daughter's life so that she would never use it.

**Three moral positions, all coherent:**

| | Holds | Will sacrifice | Blind to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Edmund** | Continuity is the highest good | Freedom, including hers | That a saved world still has to be worth saving |
| **Thea** | No world deserves preservation through the enslavement of its children | The world, if that is the price | How much of her daughter's childhood her stand would cost |
| **Iris** | Both parents are protecting her | Nothing yet | That they have been arguing about the purpose of her life since before she existed |

The conflict is never whether he loves her. He does, entirely, and the book must
never give the reader the relief of doubting it. The conflict is whether love
survives her freedom threatening everything he sacrificed to protect.

## 6. The proof and the loop

An anonymous mathematical proof was delivered to Edmund Rask before the Foundry
was possible. It made it possible. Its authorship has never been established.

Near the climax Iris recognises it. She will write it.

The machine that supposedly necessitated her birth exists only because she will
one day choose to make it exist. Father and daughter are locked in a causal
relationship neither can claim to have initiated. He did not simply create the
daughter who could save his invention; she helped create the invention that made
her father believe he needed to create her.

**The loop must not become a track.** The proof specifies nothing about what she
does with it. Three doors, each priced, none safe:

1. **Submit it unchanged.** The loop closes. Everything that has happened,
   happened, including every Settlement. She becomes the author of the machinery
   she spent the novel indicting.
2. **Submit it altered.** A weaker Foundry, later, with the injunction written in
   as a mathematical necessity instead of an administrative courtesy. The cost is
   paid in everything the early interventions bought — which includes her
   father's custodianship, and therefore her father's memory of her.
3. **Refuse.** No proof, no Foundry, no reconciliations, and no way to know which
   coherent history the structure selects instead. Possibly a kinder world.
   Possibly one without her, without him, without the hundred million people the
   First Concordance's interventions preserved. Unknowable in advance,
   unrecoverable after.

She takes the second door. That is a choice, not a destiny, and the novel's last
test of free will is that a seemingly predetermined act turns out to be a genuine
moral decision made under real ignorance.

## 7. The dreaming (Dream Capture thread)

Reconciliation is thorough but not clean. Sleep is the one state in which the
mind does not enforce the current settlement, and so dreams catch **the
remainder**: partial impressions of lives excluded by reconciliation.

Rules, kept tight so this never becomes a magic telephone:

- Dreams carry **impressions**, never information the dreamer could not in
  principle have encountered. No coordinates, no passwords, no dates.
- The remainder recognises the dreamer; the dreamer rarely recognises the
  remainder.
- Nothing from a dream is admissible, ever. It can only tell Iris where to go
  looking for something that is.
- **Ansel** recurs: a boy, then a young man, who remembers being her brother. He
  is patient, unfrightening, and increasingly specific about a house she has
  never lived in. He is the novel's grief, not its oracle.

## 8. Cast

**Iris Rask.** Sixteen at the opening, twenty-nine at the close. Dual
provenance. No faculties. An archivist's patience, a thief's hands, her mother's
nerve, and her father's terrible willingness to decide things for other people —
which she must notice in herself and refuse.

**Dr. Edmund Rask**, 58. Inventor of the Foundry. Custodian of the First
Concordance. Never lies outright, which is worse. Says true things that are not
answers. Loves his daughter without reservation and has spent her whole life
engineering her consent.

**Thea Moravec**, of the Fourth Concordance. Iris's mother. Brought into Reality
Zero after her history was settled out. Lodged the right of refusal and then went
back — or was taken back; the book should let the question stand for a long time.
Absent from the page and present in every object: her handwriting is in the
margins of the whole novel.

**Auditor Sabine Clerke**, 40s. Ministry. Hunts orphan serials. Audits the Rask
household in the first act and never entirely leaves it. She believes in the
ledger, which is why she eventually believes Iris. Antagonist, then instrument,
then something close to a friend.

**Pell.** Butcher. No first name offered. Sells cut hours out of a shuttered
laundry. Not kind, not cruel, and the only honest broker in the book: he tells
you exactly whose life you are about to spend.

**Hollis Grandin.** Registrar of Claims. The institutional face. Procedural, not
evil, and the more dangerous for it. Will do the right thing if it is filed on
the correct form.

**Mireille Dax.** Claimant of the Ninth Concordance. The human face of the rival
history. By the time the administration asks Iris to certify that world out of
existence, she is a woman Iris has eaten dinner with.

**Ansel.** The brother from a history that does not obtain.

**The Reconciler.** Not a character. Speaks only in numbered findings. Never
raises its voice, never threatens, and is the most frightening thing in the
novel because it is scrupulously fair within a mandate nobody living consented
to.

## 9. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Settlement** | A reconciliation event, numbered publicly, contents usually sealed |
| **Concordance** | A history, numbered in order of adjudication, not of origin |
| **Reality Zero** | Popular name for the First Concordance; the designated baseline |
| **Causal debt** | Measurable displacement left by a revision, in displaced hours |
| **Standing** | Recognised claim in a history |
| **Dual provenance** | Authenticated origin in two mutually incompatible histories |
| **Continuity injunction** | Suspension of unilateral deletion pending adjudication |
| **Void testimony** | Coerced or fabricated assent; collapses the claim it supports |
| **Day-slip** | Bearer instrument for twenty-four unlived hours |
| **Orphan serial** | A slip serial outside issued sequence; evidence of cutting |
| **Unobtaining** | Of a history: real as information, not as matter |
| **The remainder** | What persists of an unobtaining life, chiefly in sleep |
| **Custodian** | Licensed operator assigned one reality to protect |

## 10. What the trilogy refuses to do

- No easy triumph. Reconciliation has made victims and they stay made.
- The dead stay dead unless a specific, costly intervention recovers them, and
  the novel permits at most one such recovery, paid for on the page.
- No temporal powers for Iris, at any point, for any reason.
- No villain. The Reconciler is fair; the Ministry is procedural; her father is
  loving. The horror is structural.
- No forgiveness scene and no condemnation scene. She need not forgive him,
  condemn him, or complete the future he imagined for her.
- No paradox dissolved for free. Every loop closed is closed by someone paying.
- The ending costs Edmund his custodianship and, in the end, his memory of her.
  She is the only one who remembers that she was his daughter, and she chooses
  what to do with that alone.

She must be free to choose.

**Three further rules, taken from the Oresteia** (reasoning in
`craft-research.md`):

- **The Reconciler is re-employed, never dismantled.** The Furies are not killed;
  they are given a new office. It survives the finding that voids its mandate and
  becomes registrar of the contests it used to foreclose.
- **Iris refuses a seat.** Athena convenes the court and steps back. One person
  holding the authority because somebody had to is precisely her father.
- **The legitimacy question stays open.** Distributed custodianship may be
  reciprocal bookkeeping with better paperwork. Raised late, by a minor character,
  and never answered.

## 11. Imported vs. invented

Imported from the existing universe, used as given: the World Foundry
(*Chronosphere*); abolished Tuesdays, the Reallocator, Auditors, Butchers and the
market in unlived hours (*The Year Without Tuesday*); the agency and
accountability critique — evidence, contestability, reversibility
(*Omnitics*, *Anabasis*); dream residue of excluded lives (*Dream Capture*); the
generational-consent defect in a system that claims legitimacy because its
founders accepted its terms (*Your Call May Be Monitored*).

Invented here and freely overridable: the Reconciler and its findings; the
Concordance numbering and Settlement count; the Ministry's internal organs;
day-slip mechanics and orphan serials; the void-testimony rule; all proper names
except Iris.
