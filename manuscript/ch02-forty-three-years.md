# HEIR ÆTERNA · BOOK ONE: THE INHERITANCE

## Chapter Two — Forty-Three Years

She carried it for two days the way you carry a tooth that has started to go:
aware of it at every moment, and touching it only when nobody was looking.

On the first day it rode in the geography folder against her back, between the
cardboard and the last page of her notebook, and she did not take the folder out
of her satchel in any lesson where she could avoid it. In geography itself, which
could not be avoided, she drew the eastern coast for Mrs. Halloran with the folder
held shut by the flat of her left hand, and got eight out of ten, and lost the two
marks for the strait, which she had drawn too narrow. She looked at the strait
for some time after the book came back. North of it, on Mrs. Halloran's map, there
was sea.

At lunch Bo Teague asked whether she was ill.

"No."

"You've eaten one crisp."

"I'm not hungry."

"Nobody's ever not hungry for crisps," said Bo, with the authority of a boy who
had made a study of it, and pushed the bag across the bench to her, and she took
a second one so that he would stop looking at her face. He had a way of looking
that was entirely friendly and missed nothing, like a dog in a kitchen. She had
liked it for four years. On the first day of the letter it was unbearable.

That night her father said, "Three," and she said, "Go on, then," and when he got
to the third question she said *nothing* in the same voice she had always said
it, and found that it was easier the second time. That frightened her more than
the letter did. She had thought lying would be a thing you got worse at, the way
you get worse at a sum the longer you stare at it. It was not. It was a thing
you got better at, and fast, and nobody told you that.

---

She had reasons for not opening it, and she went over them in bed like a girl
counting coins she already knew the total of.

She did not open it because she wanted to be somewhere safe when she did, and
there was nowhere safe. She did not open it because her father might come up the
stairs. She did not open it because the envelope itself was evidence, and she had
not finished with the envelope.

That last one was at least partly true. She had spent most of a free period in
the school library with it laid flat inside an open copy of *Rivers of the
Eastern Seaboard*, examining the outside with the brass reading glass that hung on
a chain from the newspaper stand. The stamp was printed by some process she did
not know, the green laid down in fine diagonal lines rather than dots, and the
bridge on it was a suspension bridge with four towers, which was two more towers
than any bridge she had ever seen. The perforations had been torn by hand, a
little raggedly on the left. Somebody had licked it. The postmark had been struck
with a steel die rather than a rubber one — she could see the bite of it in the
paper, the way she had felt the bite of the letterpress on the slip — and the date
was the date, and did not change no matter how close she held the glass.

The paper of the envelope was heavier than anything the Ministry used and had no
seal in it when she tilted it to the window. It had instead a faint watermark of
a bird she could not identify, its wings raised, and a word under the bird in the
same small capitals as the country on the stamp.

She wrote all of this down in the back of the geography notebook under the
serial, in a list, with the date and the time of each observation, because that
was what you did with evidence, and because writing it down was something to do
with her hands that was not opening the letter.

The real reason was simpler, and she knew it, and she did not write it down.

As long as it was shut, it might say anything. It might be a mistake, or a joke,
or a cruelty somebody had gone to enormous trouble over. It might be for a
different Iris. Once she had read it she would know which, and there would be no
getting back to the version of herself who did not know, because that was how
knowing worked, and she had been raised by a man whose entire profession was the
study of what could and could not be got back.

---

On the second day, which was Thursday, she understood something about the
postmark that she should have seen at once.

It was forty-three years from Thursday. Not from the day it arrived; from this
one. Whoever had posted it had counted from today, and had sent it so that it
would be in her hands by now, and had therefore known — to the day, from forty-
three years off — when it would come to rest in a geography folder and how long
she would leave it there.

She was in the queue for the tram when that arrived in her, and she let two trams
go.

---

She opened it at a quarter to midnight on Thursday, at her desk, with the lamp
pulled down low and her cardigan stuffed along the bottom of the door so that the
light would not show on the landing.

She did not tear it. She took the butter knife she had carried up in her sleeve
after supper and worked it along under the flap, a quarter-inch at a time,
because the envelope was evidence and she wanted it whole. The gum gave easily.
It was old gum, or it had been sealed by someone who had expected it to be opened
carefully and had not pressed hard.

Inside was a single sheet of the same heavy paper, folded in thirds, with one
corner of the top third turned down a quarter of an inch.

She looked at that corner for a long time before she unfolded anything.

Iris turned down the corners of things. Not books — her father had cured her of
books at seven — but notes, lists, the slips of paper she put inside other
things. A quarter-inch, top right, so that she could get a fingernail under it
later. She did it without thinking. She had done it on Monday night to the page at the
back of the geography notebook where she copied out the serial, so that she could
find it again in the dark, and it was turned down still.

She unfolded the sheet.

It was mostly empty. That was the first thing, and in some ways it was the worst:
a whole page of that beautiful paper, and at the top of it, in the hand from the
envelope, three lines, with a great deal of white below them, as though the
writer had considered saying more and had decided, with some effort, against it.

> Iris,
>
> Your father is not the first inventor of time travel.
>
> Never let him correct the mistake he calls your existence.

There was no signature.

She found that she had put her hand flat on the desk beside the page, the way
you steady yourself on a tram.

---

She read it, in the end, perhaps forty times. She read it until the words stopped
being words and turned into shapes and then turned back into words again and
meant something slightly different each time they came back.

*Time travel.* Nobody said that. It was a wireless word, a word from the serials
that came on after the news on Saturdays, men in brass helmets stepping out of
cabinets into the Roman wars. Her father had never once used it in her hearing,
not even to laugh at it. At the Foundry they said *revision*, or *reconciliation*,
or they said that an operator had *stood in* a history, the way you would stand in
for an absent colleague at a meeting. Iris knew that much about his work and not
a great deal more, the way you know your father's work: that he had built the
Foundry, with others, before she was born; that it was the reason the Ministry
existed in the shape it did; that he went there five days in six and came home
smelling of hot metal and was not to be asked about it at the table.

Whoever had written the letter knew all the proper words. That was obvious from
the hand alone; this was not someone who learned things off the wireless. They
had chosen the child's word anyway. They had chosen it, Iris thought, for the
reader they had in mind, the way you speak more simply to someone who is going to
be frightened.

*Not the first.* Then there had been someone before him. Somebody had built it,
or begun to, and her father had — what? Come second, and been credited first?
Taken it? She tried to imagine him taking anything and could not. She could only
imagine him finding something already there, on a shelf he had been able to reach
for years, and picking it up with both arms like a sleeping animal.

And the second line.

She made herself read it slowly, a word at a time, the way she had been taught to
read a serial.

*Never let him correct.* She knew what a correction was. Everybody did. A
correction was what the Registry issued when the record had been found to be in
error, a buff form with the circle-and-number seal and a line of print at the
bottom that said *The previous entry is withdrawn and should not be relied upon.*
People received them in the post. Her father had once received one about the
number of the house, which had apparently been a seven all along rather than a
nine, and he had gone out that same afternoon in the rain and unscrewed the brass
nine from the door and screwed up a seven he had bought on the way home, and she
had watched him from the window, and thought nothing of it at all.

*The mistake he calls your existence.*

So he called it that. Somewhere — to himself, to somebody, in a place she had
never been — her father called her existence a mistake. And the person who had
written this letter knew he did, and knew what a man with his profession could do
about a mistake, and had sent the warning back across forty-three years in the
plainest words they had.

She sat with that until the lamp started to tick as it cooled. She found it did
not hurt in the way she would have expected. It hurt the way a cold room hurts,
everywhere at once and in no particular place, so that she could not have put a
finger on it.

He laid the table with two of everything. He had answered her questions for an
hour, at eight, with the lamp on.

She did not know how to hold those things and the letter in the same hand. She
held them anyway. She had been practising, since Monday.

---

At half past one she got up and took down her exercise books.

There were a great many of them. She had kept every one since she was six, not
out of sentiment but because nobody had ever told her to throw them away, and they
stood in a long row on the shelf above her bed in order of year, their spines
gone soft and bulging. She took down the last four and opened them on the floor in
a fan around the letter, and knelt there with the lamp on the boards beside her,
and began.

She did it the way the posters taught you to check a banknote: not by looking at
the whole thing, which any forger could get right, but by looking at the places
a forger would not think to look.

The capital I was too narrow, in the letter and in every book. That proved
nothing. Anyone with a sample could narrow an I.

The loop on the second s in *Rask* on the envelope was closed, which was a habit
of hers she had been trying to break since she was ten. That proved nothing
either. It was in every exercise book she owned, on the cover, in her name.

She went on.

She was looking for something that was not in the books, and she found the first
of it in the *t*s.

Iris crossed her *t*s last. She had always done it. She wrote a whole word, and
then went back and put the bars across, quickly, all at once, so that each bar ran
a little long and nicked the top of the following letter. Miss Fenwick had marked
her down for it in the second form and she had tried to stop and failed, and in
the end it had simply become the way her writing looked, though nobody looking at
a finished page would know why it looked that way. You could not see the order of
it. You could only see it if you knew to put a glass on the place where the bar
met the next letter and look at which ink lay on top.

She did not have the library glass. She had the lens from the back of her father's
old pocket torch, which she had taken apart when she was twelve and never put
together again, and it was enough. In *first*, in *inventor*, in *travel*, in
*let*, in *correct*: the bar of every *t* lay over the stroke beside it. Every one.
It had been written a word at a time and then gone back over, the way she wrote,
by someone who had gone on writing that way for forty more years and never been
cured of it.

That was the first thing. A forger could have copied the shape of her *t*s from
any exercise book on the shelf. He could not have copied the order in which she
made them, because the order was not on the page unless you went looking for it,
and nobody but Iris had ever had a reason to go looking.

The second thing she found was in the word *your*.

It came twice. *Your father. Your existence.* And both times the *y* was not her
*y*.

It was her mother's.

Not quite — older, steadier, the tail of it shorter than the one in the atlas —
but the same letter in its bones. It leaned forward. Its tail did not stop at the
bottom of its own stroke but swept on into the *o* without the pen lifting, in a
hurry, as if the writing were only a transcription and the speaker had moved on.

Iris sat back on her heels.

At twelve, for about five months, in secret, she had tried to make her writing go
like her mother's. She had got nowhere. She had stopped. She had never told
anybody she had tried. And out of all those months of copying one line of a
photograph's back, the only thing that had stayed with her was that *y*. It came
out when she wrote quickly and for nobody — in lists, on her hand, in the margins
of things that would be thrown away. It had never once got into an exercise book.
She had been careful of that without ever deciding to be. She sat in the classroom
and wrote *your* upright and separate and slow, and at home, alone, writing fast,
her mother's *y* ran forward into the *o* as if it had somewhere to be.

She looked at the back of the geography notebook, where on Monday night she had
copied out the serial and the note. *This was the day you were born.* She had
written it slowly, under the lamp, and the *y* in *you* stood upright.

Then she looked at the list of observations below it, the one she had made in the
library that afternoon in a hurry, with the free period nearly over. *Stamp: four
towers. Perforations torn by hand, left side ragged. Your glass doesn't* — she had
written that, to herself, meaning the library glass — and there it was. The *y*
leaning into the *o*. Her mother's letter, in her own hand, in a note to nobody.

There was not a forger in the world who could have known to put that there. There
was not a person in the world who had ever seen it except the one kneeling on the
floor.

And one other.

---

She did not sleep. She did not try very hard.

At some point after three she found herself thinking not about the letter but
about its writer — not the words but the hand, which had had forty-three more
years than hers and had used them to grow economical, so that the letters had the
compression of a signature. A person who still turned the corners down. A person
who had never been cured of the *t*s. A person who, at some point between Iris and
herself, had stopped hiding the *y*, because the word *your* had gone onto that
heavy paper twice in her mother's shape, carefully, with no hurry in it at all.

She thought: she knew I would check.

She thought: she put it there for me to find.

It was not a comfort, exactly. But it was the first time since Monday that she
had felt anything like company.

She had been keeping secrets from her father for three days. She had thought,
until tonight, that she was keeping them alone.

At four she folded the letter back into thirds along its own creases and turned
the corner down again, top right, a quarter of an inch, and put it inside the
envelope, and put the envelope flat between the pages of the oldest exercise book
on the shelf — the first form, the year she was six, where the handwriting was
enormous and wobbling and every *t* was crossed last and nobody would ever think
to look for anything.

Then she lay down on top of the covers in her clothes and watched the window go
from black to the colour of continuity stock.

Downstairs at seven the wireless came on, and her father said something to it,
mildly, the way he did.

At breakfast he said, "Three."

"Go on, then."

He asked whether she had slept, and she said yes. He asked whether she had
finished her geography, and she said yes, and that was true. He buttered his toast
to all four corners, with enormous attention, and then he asked the third
question.

"Did anything happen."

Iris looked at him across the table. At his long hands, and the grey at his
temples and nowhere else, and the second cup laid out for her without his
thinking about it, the way he had laid it out every morning of her life. She
loved him. It was the one fact in the house that had not changed since Monday,
and she held it next to the other one, the one in the first-form exercise book,
and found that they did not touch each other at any point.

"Nothing," she said.

She had a correspondent now. She had someone, forty-three years off, who knew
exactly what she had just said to him, and why, and how easily it had come — who
had sat at this table and said it herself, once, in this voice, and had kept the
secret the whole long way from here to there.

And had not told him either.
