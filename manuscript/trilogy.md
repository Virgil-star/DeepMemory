# HEIR ÆTERNA

## A trilogy — series architecture

*Heir Æterna* — the eternal heir, and the heir to eternity. Iris Rask inherits a
world she never agreed to, and over three books she stops being its instrument
and becomes its author.

> Revised against the craft research in `craft-research.md`. The fourteen adopted
> changes are marked **[C1]**–**[C14]** where they land.

---

## The three books

**[C5]** Titled as three legal instruments, escalating by what Iris *is* in each.
One register, three steps, and the middle title indicts her father in a single
word.

| | Book | Question | She is | Antagonist | She becomes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | **The Inheritance** | *What am I?* | what she receives | her father's tenderness | an **heir** |
| **II** | **The Instrument** | *What was I made for?* | what she is used as | her father's programme | an **executor** |
| **III** | **The Appeal** | *What am I going to do about it?* | what she files | the mandate itself | an **author** |

Heir, executor, author. The series title is the whole arc in two words, and each
book ends with her accepting a role she did not ask for and will not hand back.

---

## The cost ladder

A trilogy fails when the middle volume is a holding pattern. Each book takes
something she cannot get back, and the three losses escalate in kind rather than
in size.

- **Book One costs her the house.** Not her father — the *trust*. She betrays him
  before he betrays her, knowingly, to buy her own immunity, at sixteen.
  Everything afterward happens to someone who already knows what she is capable of.
- **Book Two costs her the mother.** Not a death scene; the end of the possibility
  of rescue. Thea has been dead the whole time. What she left is not a hiding
  place but a workload.
- **Book Three costs her the father.** His custodianship, and then his memory of
  her. He survives. He is courteous. He does not know her name.

Trust, then hope, then him.

**[C13] The form strips with the supports.** Book Three runs shortest, with the
fewest settings, the fewest named characters, and no world-explanation of any
kind. By then the reader knows the machinery. Anything restated is a failure of
nerve.

## Tempo and tense

**[C8] Book One may be episodic. Book Two may not.** Book One is a sixteen-year-old
making discrete incursions into a world — chapters loosely strung, each one a
raid. Book Two is driven from its first page by a single escalating line of
pursuit. This resolves the middle-volume problem structurally rather than by
exhortation.

**[C6] Book Two opens on a time-marker object.** Not a summary of the missing
three years — a day-slip with a **sequential** serial, issued stock, bought over a
counter with a receipt in hand. One object says that the girl who stole a cut slip
out of an atlas now transacts inside the system.

**[C7] Late compression.** The settlement sequence in Book Three may pass years in
a page, and should. Book One's slow episodic autumn is what buys that right.

**[C12] Past tense as testimony.** Close third on Iris, past tense, never leaving
her vantage, in all three volumes. The retrospective voice here is not knowingness
— it is **deposition**. The trilogy builds toward her giving evidence on the
record, and the narration should read as the evidence.

Present tense is reserved, strictly, for two registers: the Reconciler's numbered
findings, quoted as documents, and the settlement sequences where history is
actively being chosen. Perhaps six uses across three books. When the prose slips
into present, the past has come unfixed.

## Who knows what, and when

The trilogy's single greatest risk is spending a reveal early.

**Book One may reveal**
- She was born on an abolished day; her birth record says so.
- Her mother came from an unobtaining history.
- Her father made her deliberately, as an obstacle to deletion.
- Her mother imposed an independently enforceable right of refusal, and a second
  clause he did not know about.

**Book One must withhold**
- That Reality Zero is a designation rather than a provenance.
- That her father has been engineering her consent for eleven years.
- That Thea is dead.
- That other dual-provenance children exist.
- Every mention of the proof.

**Book Two may reveal**
- The programme: eleven years of arranged circumstances.
- The others — children of impossible histories, most of whom signed when asked.
- That a provenance can be successfully challenged as fabricated, which means her
  standing has a hole in it.
- Reality Zero is a filing order. Her father mistook an assignment for an
  entitlement.
- Thea is dead, and left a testimony in trust.
- The Ninth Concordance's claim is stronger. The certification demand.

**Book Two must withhold**
- The proof and its authorship. The furthest Book Two may go is the one thing her
  father will not say, named as a withholding and never explained.

**Book Three reveals**
- The proof is in her own hand and she has not written it yet.
- The mandate rests on inherited consent and is therefore void by the system's
  own rule.
- The price of each of the three doors, and which one she walks through.

## The ending, revised

**[C1] The Reconciler is re-employed, not destroyed.** The Furies are not killed;
they are given a new office. The Reconciler survives the finding that voids its
mandate and is reassigned as registrar of the very contests it used to foreclose
— the most feared instrument in the world, still scrupulous, now working for the
claimants. Nothing is wasted and nothing is forgiven.

**[C2] Iris refuses a seat.** Athena convenes the court and then steps back. Iris
was drifting toward becoming the arbiter of the new system, which would make her
precisely her father: one person holding the authority because somebody had to.
She files, she establishes standing for others, and she declines the office. The
refusal of power is her last and hardest act, and the one thing he could never
do.

**[C3] The legitimacy question stays open.** Distributed custodianship is not
justice achieved. The last movement admits the possibility that it is reciprocal
bookkeeping with better paperwork, and declines to settle it. One line of doubt,
placed late, never answered.

**[C14] The authorship fold is executed without gloss.** She reads the proof and
recognises the hand. No character explains the loop to the reader, in any volume,
at any point. Cut every such paragraph on sight.

## Connective tissue that is not plot

**[C9] The atlas is the recurring object.** The oversized pre-Settlement atlas
with the boundaries of unobtaining countries drawn in gray. Book One chapter 1: it
is robbed. Book Two: it is consulted. Book Three: it is entered as evidence. Coda:
it lies open on his table. Four appearances, no symbolism announced.

**[C4] The converging claimants.** Three apparently minor figures, one introduced
per volume, appear together in Book Three as named claimants on the docket: the
woman in the tenement whose month Iris spent in Book One, Bo Teague from Book
One's ordinary world, and Mireille Dax of the Ninth Concordance from Book Two. The
reader has met each as local colour. They turn out to be the case. Nothing is
announced — they simply appear on a list, in order, and the list is the payoff.

**[C10] The cycle closes line for line.** The coda rhymes with chapter one almost
phrase for phrase, gesture inverted: she stole the note out of the binding at
sixteen, and at the end she puts it back and leaves it. Same atlas, same hand,
opposite act.

### The letter, across three books

The forty-three-year letter in Book One chapter 2 is the series' load-bearing
object. It says two things: *your father is not the first inventor of time
travel*, and *never let him correct the mistake he calls your existence.*

- **Book One** reads it as a warning about her father. Chapter 37 re-reads it and
  catches the problem: he has never once called her a mistake.
- **Book Two** reads it as a warning about the administration. Someone in a file
  called her a mistake. She goes looking for the file.
- **Book Three** reads it correctly. *Not the first inventor* is not an accusation
  about a rival — it is a statement about authorship. The "mistake he calls her
  existence" is a phrase from his own contemporaneous notes, written before he
  loved her, which she will eventually find and which he will never remember
  writing.

Quoted identically in all three books. Reader-detectable, and it rewards the
reread.

## The father's curve

He is never a villain and he is never redeemed.

- **Book One:** a loving man with evasions. He never lies outright, which is
  worse. He says true things that are not answers.
- **Book Two:** a loving man with a programme. The arrangements are real, the care
  is real, and he cannot tell the difference — because to him they are one act.
- **Book Three:** a loving man on the other side of the table. He testifies
  against her because preserving his world requires it, and he undercuts his own
  testimony where only she is close enough to notice, and will not admit to it
  afterward.

He is right about the thing he is right about, in all three books: nobody gave him
the authority, somebody had to hold it, and the deleted were not consulted either.
The series never takes that away from him.

## Standalone obligations

Each book closes its own question so that none is a hostage to the next.

- **Book One** answers *what am I* completely. The sequel hook is not a withheld
  answer; it is a decision she declines to make yet.
- **Book Two** answers *what was I made for* completely, including the part her
  father never intended: she was made to be an obstacle, and her mother made her
  an executor instead. **[C11]** It ends with her standing suspended on a
  fabrication challenge and *then* the demand arriving — so the administration
  requires a certification from a woman it has just ruled may not exist. Less
  stable than it began, which is the whole of the rule.
- **Book Three** answers the last question and pays every outstanding bill,
  including the ones the reader has forgotten: the woman in the tenement, Bo
  Teague's family, the orphan serial from Book One chapter 9.

**The seam between volumes is a wound, not a hinge.** Book One resolves its
question informationally and resolves nothing emotionally.

---

## What the trilogy refuses

- No easy triumph in any volume. Reconciliation has made victims and they stay
  made.
- No temporal powers for Iris at any point, in any book, for any reason. She is
  *admissible*, not special.
- No villain. The Reconciler is scrupulously fair within a mandate nobody living
  consented to. That is the horror.
- No middle volume that exists to delay the third.
- No forgiveness scene and no condemnation scene, in any volume.
- The dead stay dead. The trilogy permits exactly one costly recovery across all
  three books, and it is not her mother.
